Student debt in the US has crossed $1.7 trillion. This financial burden delays home ownership, family formation, and wealth building for an entire generation, creating long-term economic drag.

Countries with free or heavily subsidized higher education — Germany, Norway, Finland — consistently produce highly educated workforces and competitive economies without the debt crisis seen in the US.

Education is a public good. A more educated population produces better public health outcomes, lower crime rates, higher civic participation, and greater economic productivity for everyone.

Free college removes the financial barrier that prevents talented low-income students from accessing higher education, making meritocracy more genuine rather than a function of family wealth.

Free college universalizes a benefit that disproportionately flows to people who would have gone to college anyway — the relatively privileged. It does little for those who need vocational training or can't attend full time.

Someone has to pay. Funding free college through taxation means lower and middle class taxpayers subsidize degrees for people who will earn significantly more than them over their lifetimes.

Without tuition revenue, universities face funding pressure that could reduce quality, research output, and the range of programs available. Free doesn't mean costless — it means costs shift elsewhere.

Credential inflation is already a problem. Making college free could accelerate the trend of employers requiring degrees for jobs that don't genuinely need them, devaluing degrees further.

Capitalism has produced the greatest reduction in global poverty in human history. The number of people living in extreme poverty has fallen from 36% in 1990 to under 10% today, driven primarily by market economies.

Price signals in free markets efficiently allocate resources based on real demand. No central planner has the information capacity to replicate what millions of decentralized market decisions achieve spontaneously.

Competition under capitalism drives innovation. The technology, medicine, and infrastructure improvements of the last two centuries were overwhelmingly produced by market incentives and private investment.

Property rights and rule of law — the foundations of capitalism — create stability and predictability that attract investment, enable long-term planning, and build functioning institutions.

Capitalism produces extreme inequality. In the US, the top 1% holds more wealth than the bottom 50% combined. A system that generates this concentration of power undermines the democratic equality it claims to support.

Markets fail in systematic ways — monopoly formation, environmental externalities, public goods underproduction, information asymmetry. These failures require significant non-market intervention to correct.

Capitalism's growth imperative is structurally incompatible with ecological sustainability. An economic system requiring perpetual expansion on a finite planet is mathematically unsustainable.

Labor under capitalism is systematically undervalued relative to capital. Workers who produce value receive wages while owners who provide capital receive profits — a structural power imbalance that compounds over time.

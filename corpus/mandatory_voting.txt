Australia has had compulsory voting since 1924 with turnout consistently above 90%. The result is a parliament that must appeal to the entire population, not just motivated partisan bases.

Low voter turnout systematically underrepresents young people, poor people, and minorities. Mandatory voting structurally corrects this bias and produces governments that reflect the actual population.

When voting is optional, parties invest resources in mobilizing their base rather than persuading the center. Mandatory voting forces campaigns to appeal broadly, reducing polarization.

The cost of not voting is low — a small fine in most countries with compulsory voting. The benefit is a democracy that is structurally more representative and harder to manipulate through voter suppression.

Forcing citizens to vote violates political freedom. The right not to vote — to withhold consent from a political system — is as fundamental as the right to vote itself.

Compulsory voting doesn't produce more informed voters — it produces more reluctant ones. Uninformed or disengaged voters choosing randomly can distort election results in unpredictable ways.

Low turnout is a symptom, not the disease. If people aren't voting it's because they feel unrepresented. Forcing them to the booth doesn't fix the underlying problem of political alienation.

Enforcement of mandatory voting is costly and creates a bureaucratic burden. Tracking, fining, and processing millions of non-voters requires significant government resources that could be spent elsewhere.

Social media platforms expose children to cyberbullying, predatory behavior, and harmful content. Young minds are not equipped to handle the psychological pressure of public scrutiny, likes, and comparison culture.

Research from the Royal Society for Public Health found that Instagram, Snapchat, and TikTok are linked to increased rates of anxiety, depression, and poor body image among teenagers.

The teenage brain is still developing impulse control and emotional regulation. Constant social media exposure interferes with this development and creates dependency patterns similar to addiction.

Platforms deliberately use addictive design — infinite scroll, notifications, variable rewards — to maximize engagement. These mechanisms disproportionately harm younger users who lack the maturity to resist them.

A ban would be unenforceable in practice. Teenagers will simply use VPNs or their parents' accounts. Prohibition without digital literacy education creates more harm than it prevents.

Social media is how young people organize politically, access mental health communities, and stay connected with peers. Banning it removes a vital communication tool without addressing the root causes of harm.

The solution is better platform regulation and digital literacy education, not blanket bans. Teaching young people to use social media critically is more sustainable than prohibition.

Parental controls and platform-level age verification tools already exist. Enforcement should be on platforms, not imposed as a legal restriction on users.
